The Hungry Orcas of the Salish Sea

The ecosystem

The Salish Sea

The Salish Sea is the inland sea shared by Washington State (USA) and British Columbia (Canada). It includes Puget Sound, the Strait of Georgia and the Strait of Juan de Fuca. Cold, nutrient-rich water supports huge blooms of phytoplankton, which feed a food web of zooplankton, herring, salmon, seals and orcas. Rivers such as the Fraser and the Columbia link the sea to forests and mountains, because salmon hatch in rivers, grow up in the ocean, and return to rivers to spawn.

Sources: NOAA Fisheries; US EPA Salish Sea program.

The focus species

Southern Resident killer whale (Orcinus orca)

Southern Residents live in three family groups called pods: J, K and L. They are apex predators, so nothing hunts them. Unlike other orcas that eat seals, Southern Residents are specialists: most of their diet is Chinook salmon, the largest and fattiest salmon. As apex predators they are an indicator species: if they are sick, it tells scientists the whole food web below them is in trouble.

The ecological issue

The population has not recovered since it was listed as endangered. Scientists from NOAA identify three main threats: not enough Chinook salmon to eat, toxic pollutants such as PCBs stored in the whales' fat, and noise and disturbance from boats that makes hunting harder. This audit investigates how these threats are connected, and what could be done about them.

Section 2 · Population data & food web

A population stuck below its carrying capacity

Every summer, researchers photograph each whale and identify it by its dorsal fin and saddle patch. This gives an exact count, not an estimate.

Southern Resident population, 2006–2026

Data: annual July 1 census by the Center for Whale Research, reported by NOAA and the Puget Sound Partnership. Hover or tap a point to read its value.

Reading the trend

Over 20 years the population fell from 89 whales (2006) to 76 (2026), a drop of 13 whales or about 15%. The decline was not a straight line. Numbers stayed between 85 and 88 until 2012, then fell sharply to 72 in 2020, the lowest point in the graph. Since 2020 the population has only wobbled between 72 and 76.

The dashed line marks an estimated carrying capacity (K) of about 86 whales, the largest population the current Chinook supply seems able to support. The population reached or passed this line only until 2011 and has stayed below it ever since.

Limiting factors
Density-dependent: food supply. All 76 whales compete for the same Chinook salmon. When Chinook runs are small, each whale gets less food, fewer females become pregnant and more calves die. The bigger the population, the stronger this effect.
Density-independent: warm ocean water. A marine heatwave (nicknamed "the Blob") warmed the Pacific in 2014–2016 and reduced the food available to young salmon. This affects the whales no matter how many there are. The graph shows the drop from 83 (2016) to 72 (2020) followed it.
Prediction. If Chinook numbers rose by about 25%, K would rise too. The whales would have food to spare for pregnancies, and the count could climb back toward 86, NOAA's recovery goal for 2030. If Chinook keep falling, K drops and the population is likely to keep sliding.

Math check: the 10% rule

Only about 10% of the energy at one level is stored in the next level. The other 90% is used for life processes (movement, keeping warm, growth) or lost as heat and waste.

Only 0.01% of the producer energy reaches an orca. That is why there are so few orcas, and why they need a huge salmon population beneath them. An adult orca needs roughly 25 Chinook salmon a day, so even a small fall in salmon numbers has a big effect at the top.

Noise simulator

Can the orca still hear its dinner?

Orcas find salmon by echolocation: they send out clicks and listen for echoes. Move the boat closer and watch the hunting range shrink as engine noise drowns out the echoes.

Simplified model for illustration. Washington State law now requires boats to stay 1,000 yards from Southern Residents.

How the threats link together. Habitat destruction and overharvesting both reduce Chinook salmon. Hungry whales burn their blubber for energy, which releases stored PCBs into their blood. Boat noise makes it even harder to find the few salmon left. So the threats multiply each other: this is why the population has not recovered, and why the loss of an apex predator reduces the stability of the whole ecosystem.
Section 4 · Pollution & toxins

PCBs: a poison that climbs the food chain

PCBs (polychlorinated biphenyls) are industrial chemicals once used in electrical equipment, paints and sealants. The US banned them in 1979, but they break down extremely slowly and are still washing into the Salish Sea today.

1 · How it enters

Rain washes PCBs from old buildings, contaminated soil and industrial sites into storm drains. Storm drains carry the water straight into rivers and Puget Sound. PCBs stick to particles and settle in sediment, where they last for decades.

2 · How organisms are exposed

Plankton absorb PCBs from water and sediment. PCBs dissolve in fat (lipids), not water, so they are not excreted. They are stored in fatty tissue. Every animal that eats contaminated prey also takes in the PCBs stored in the prey's fat.

3 · Effects

PCBs weaken the immune system (more disease), disrupt hormones and reduce reproduction. Mothers pass PCBs to calves through fat-rich milk, so a first-born calf can receive a large dose before it can hunt.

Quantifying the threat
≈ 9,000×

The PCB concentration in an orca's blubber (about 37 ppm) is roughly 9,000 times higher than in phytoplankton (about 0.004 ppm). That is 37 ÷ 0.004 ≈ 9,250. The average orca is also more than twice the 17 ppm health threshold, and some individuals measured up to 120 ppm.

Bioaccumulation vs biomagnification

Bioaccumulation happens inside one animal: an orca lives up to 50–90 years, and PCBs build up in its blubber year after year.

Biomagnification happens across the food chain: each predator eats many prey, so it collects all their PCBs. This links back to the 10% rule. Energy is lost at each level, but the PCBs are not, so they become more concentrated as they move up.

The hunger link: when salmon are scarce, orcas burn blubber for energy. This releases stored PCBs into the blood, where they do the most damage.
New research · microplastics

Reading the "poop archive"

NOAA scientists follow the whales in a small boat at a safe distance and scoop up floating scat with long-handled nets. They have saved hundreds of samples collected from 2006 to 2018. Researchers at the University of Washington are now testing them for microplastics, plastic pieces 5 mm or smaller. Early tests found many plastic fibres. Microplastics make up 92% of the plastic pieces floating at the ocean surface, and they can carry toxic chemicals with them up the food chain.

Section 5 · Conservation solutions

Feed the whales first

The root cause is a lack of Chinook salmon. It drives the population decline (Section 2) and makes the PCB problem worse (Section 4). So the main plan targets salmon, supported by two strategies against the other threats.

Recovery model

What could more salmon do?

Move the slider to change the Chinook supply and see a projected population for 2036. This is a simple classroom model, not a real forecast: each extra 10% of Chinook adds about 0.6% growth a year to today's slow decline.

How ecosystem health improves. More Chinook means better-fed orcas, more successful births and less blubber burning, so less PCB released into the blood. Healthy salmon runs also feed bears, eagles and seals, and their bodies fertilise river forests when they die after spawning. Cleaner stormwater reduces toxins for every animal in the food web, not just orcas.
